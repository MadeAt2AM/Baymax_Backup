# Niche Guide: Tech / AI / Vibe Coding / Building in Public

## Audience Psychology

This niche spans several overlapping audiences:

**Tech/AI viewers:**
- Follow AI news obsessively — they want "what's NEW and what does it actually mean for me"
- Skeptical of hype but also fear missing out (FOMO is a real driver)
- Want signal over noise — there's too much AI content; why is THIS video worth watching?
- Mix of builders, learners, and observers

**Vibe Coding viewers:**
- Non-technical founders and creators who want to build without traditional coding
- Developers curious about AI-assisted workflows
- Want to see real results from real people, not polished marketing
- Respond to honesty about what works AND what breaks

**Building in Public viewers:**
- Entrepreneurially minded
- Love behind-the-scenes, raw documentation, mistakes and lessons
- Strong parasocial pull — they want to follow YOUR journey, not just learn skills
- Motivated by possibility: "if they can do it, maybe I can too"

**Universal truth for this niche:** Authenticity > polish. A slightly rough video from someone who actually built the thing beats a slick video from someone who didn't.

## Body Section Examples — Tech/AI

### Example 1: Tool Breakdown (for builder audience)

**Topic: "Claude Code vs Cursor — Which One Actually Speeds You Up"**

> The honest answer is: it depends on what kind of builder you are.
>
> Cursor is best when you know what you're building and you need an intelligent autocomplete that understands your codebase. It lives inside your existing editor. It fits the way developers already work. The autocomplete alone — Tab tab tab — is genuinely faster once you've used it for a week.
>
> Claude Code is different. It's more like having a junior engineer you can dump a task on. "Build me a user authentication flow with email verification and rate limiting." And then you come back and it's... mostly done. It writes, tests, and sometimes fixes its own mistakes in the same session.
>
> The workflows are different. Cursor is collaboration. Claude Code is delegation. And if you understand that distinction before you start, you'll pick the right tool for the right job every time.

### Example 2: Building in Public (for founder/creator audience)

**Topic: "Week 3 of Building in Public — What Actually Happened"**

> Week 3 was supposed to be the week I launched. It wasn't.
>
> Here's what happened. I had the product working. I had a landing page. I had an email list of 200 people who'd signed up for early access. And then I opened Stripe, tried to set up subscriptions, and discovered that the country I'm incorporating in has a processing delay that nobody on the internet seems to document anywhere.
>
> Five days of my launch window: gone. I'm telling you this because building in public means you get to see the actual building. Not the highlight reel. The Monday where everything broke. Here's what I did instead — and honestly, it turned out better.

### Example 3: AI Concept Explainer (for general tech audience)

**Topic: "Why Every AI Model Has a 'Personality' Now"**

> You've probably noticed that Claude sounds different from ChatGPT. Not just in what it says — in HOW it says it. More careful, more philosophical, sometimes frustratingly hedge-y. GPT-4 is punchier. Gemini feels like a slightly nervous intern.
>
> This isn't an accident. These models are trained with something called RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback. Basically, humans rate responses, and the model learns to generate more responses that get high ratings from those particular humans. So the "personality" of an AI model is literally a reflection of whose feedback shaped it.
>
> Anthropic trained Claude with a particular focus on what they call Constitutional AI — rules and values baked into the training process itself, not just the human feedback layer. That's why Claude will sometimes decline things that GPT just does. It's not a bug. It's a design choice.

## Full Skeleton Example — Tech/AI

**Video Title: "I Tried Every Major Vibe Coding Tool So You Don't Have To"**

### Hook
What to include: Most surprising conclusion from your testing
What viewer expects: Someone who's actually used all the tools, not just read the docs
How to present: Lead with the most counterintuitive finding
Reference: Name specific tools — Lovable, v0, Bolt.new, Cursor, Claude Code, Replit Agent
Example: "I gave the same project brief to six different AI coding tools. The one that finished it first wasn't the one anyone's hyping right now."

### Why This Comparison Matters
What to include: The FOMO problem — too many tools, not enough time; the cost of picking wrong
What viewer expects: Validation that this is a real problem worth solving
How to present: Quick stat or observation about how fast the landscape is changing
Reference: Number of major AI coding tools launched in the last 12 months; tool costs
Example: "Six months ago there were maybe three tools worth talking about. Now there are thirty. And they all have landing pages that make them look basically identical."

### The Test Setup
What to include: What project you used, why that project specifically, your evaluation criteria
What viewer expects: Methodological fairness — they don't want a rigged comparison
How to present: Quick and clear — don't belabor it, just establish credibility
Example: "I used the same prompt for all six tools: build a task management app with user auth, a dashboard, and Stripe subscriptions. Nothing exotic. A real thing you'd actually want to ship."

### Tool-by-Tool Breakdown (3–5 tools)
What to include: Speed, code quality, where it broke, how hard to fix the breaks, final result
What viewer expects: Honest takes — pros AND cons for each; no affiliate-shill energy
How to present: Mini-story for each tool — what happened, not just what features it has
Reference: Cursor, Claude Code, Lovable, v0, Bolt.new — use real tool names
Example (Lovable): "Lovable got me 80% of the way there faster than anything else. The UI looked great. The problem showed up when I needed to add custom logic that wasn't in its happy path — suddenly I was fighting the abstraction instead of using it."

### The Verdict — Which Tool for Which Person
What to include: Clear recommendation split by use case/skill level
What viewer expects: A direct answer — don't hedge everything
How to present: "If you're X, use Y. If you're Z, use W."
Example: "Non-technical founder who just needs something live? Lovable. Developer who wants to keep full control but go 3x faster? Cursor or Claude Code. Somewhere in between? v0 is your sweet spot."

### CTA
Example: "I'm going to spend the next 30 days building an actual product using only Claude Code — starting from zero. That build log starts right here."

## Full Script Example — Tech/AI (Short Form ~750 words)

**Title: "You're Probably Using AI Coding Tools Wrong"**

---

Most people use AI coding tools like a fancy autocomplete. Type a prompt, get some code, copy it in, move on.

And then they wonder why their codebase is a mess after three weeks.

Here's what's actually happening. AI coding tools are incredibly good at generating code that looks right. They are significantly less good at understanding what you're actually trying to build. And if you're not bridging that gap intentionally, you end up with code that technically works but has no coherent structure — and becomes impossible to extend after a certain point.

I've seen this happen to enough people now that I think it's worth talking about directly.

The fix isn't to use AI less. It's to use it differently.

The most important shift is this: stop using AI to write code. Start using AI to think about architecture.

Before you write a single line, before you even open Cursor or Claude Code, tell the AI what you're building. All of it. The full scope. Ask it to suggest a folder structure. Ask it to flag decisions that are going to matter later — auth strategy, state management, database schema. Ask it where the complexity is going to live.

Then, once you have a plan, use the AI to implement it piece by piece.

This changes everything. Instead of a random pile of AI-generated code, you get a codebase with actual intentionality. Each piece knows where it fits. The AI isn't making structural decisions for you by default — you're making them, with the AI as your thinking partner.

The second mistake I see constantly: not giving the AI enough context.

When you open a new chat and say "write me a function that does X," the AI has no idea what X lives next to, what already exists, what naming conventions you're using, or what constraints matter. So it makes assumptions. And those assumptions might be fine, or they might create an inconsistency that you'll spend two hours debugging next week.

Fix: paste in context. Paste the file you're working in. Paste your types file. Paste the component you're integrating with. Tell it what already exists. A slightly longer prompt upfront saves a massive debugging session later.

Third thing — and this one's a mindset shift — treat AI output as a first draft, not a final answer.

The people who get the most out of these tools aren't the ones who trust them most. They're the ones who read the output critically, catch the subtle issues, and ask the AI to refine. The AI will write something that looks complete. Your job is to ask "is this actually what I want?" before you move on.

This means you need to understand enough about what you're building to evaluate the output. Which is an argument for learning fundamentals even if you're vibe coding — not so you can write code without AI, but so you can tell when the AI is wrong.

Okay — so to make this concrete. Here's what the workflow actually looks like.

Step one: architecture session. New file or new feature, you start with a conversation. No code yet. Just "here's what I'm building, here's what already exists, here's what I need." Ask the AI to think through the approach with you.

Step two: implementation in slices. Small pieces, one at a time. Each with full context. Review each output before moving to the next.

Step three: reconciliation. Every hour or so, zoom out. Read the code that's been written. Ask the AI "does this make sense as a whole? Is there anything inconsistent or that should be refactored?" Let it catch its own mistakes before they compound.

Step four: testing. Ask the AI to write tests for what it just built. The act of writing tests forces it to think about edge cases it glossed over in the implementation.

That's it. Not complicated. But it's a workflow, not just "prompt and pray."

The AI tools are genuinely remarkable right now. But they're tools — and using any tool without a method produces worse results than using a worse tool with a good method.

Use the method.

## Language Tips — Tech/AI Niche

- Use real product names: Claude Code, Cursor, Lovable, v0, Bolt, Replit Agent, Copilot, Gemini, GPT-4o
- Be opinionated — wishy-washy takes don't perform in this niche; pick a side and defend it
- Respect technical vocabulary but don't gatekeep — explain on first use if the audience is mixed
- "Building in public" content should include real metrics, real mistakes, real timelines
- Avoid: "AI is going to change everything" — say specifically WHAT it changes and HOW
- "Vibe coding" is the colloquial term — use it freely in content targeted at that audience
- The audience tracks AI news closely — don't over-explain recent events they already know
- Context window, tokens, hallucination, fine-tuning, RAG — these are acceptable jargon for this audience
- Avoid model version numbers unless they're directly relevant — they go stale fast
